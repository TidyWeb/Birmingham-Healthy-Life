Notebook 01 — Source Exploration

I started with an idea that was sparked by a map of the London Tube that showed life expectancy at each station (Lives on the Line, by James Cheshire and Oliver O’Brien). Places a few stops apart had very different health outcomes, and a railway map made that easy to see. I wanted to know if Birmingham’s rail corridors would do the same. Yet, the more I looked, the more I cared about neighbouring areas that differ a lot, railway or not. So I’m going city-wide instead of line by line. The initial concept of using rail corridors and train stations as datapoints is deprecated, but the idea of looking at health outcomes in small areas is still valid. I’m using Middle Layer Super Output Areas (MSOAs) as my unit of analysis because that granularity is what the Office for National Statistics (ONS) uses for health data. There are 660 MSOAs in Birmingham and the surrounding areas.

Why healthy life expectancy

Life expectancy tells you how long people are expected to live. Healthy life expectancy (HLE) tells you how many of those years are expected to be in good health. Two areas can have a similar life expectancy and very different HLE. Large gaps are what I want to look at as I am interested in discrepancies, and then I want to see how such discrepancies line up or correlate with things like income, housing, work, education and pollution. I’m not assuming any of those explain it, but I am interested in exploring why such discrepancies exist. I want to see if there are patterns that can be explained by geography, demography, or other factors.

So HLE is my main dataset. Everything else gets compared against it later.

Who it’s for

This project is for the people of Birmingham and the West Midlands. People who know Birmingham and the places around it, both the public and professionals. They should recognise the areas by name, read the maps, and relate what they see to what they already know. The numbers describe areas. They don’t tell us the future health of any one person who lives there.

What this notebook does

I check the national HLE profile first. I use YData Profiling for that and I love it: it’s quick, and it can help catch issues early. Then I look hard at my 660 local areas: how they compare with the rest of the country, the gap between men and women, how precise the estimates are, which ones were imputed, and how much neighbouring areas differ. It finishes with what I’m taking into Notebook 02 — Data Preparation.

HLE source and definitions: ONS MSOA health-state life expectancy, 2019–2023.

Neighbouring areas

This is the question I started with: where do areas next to each other have very different HLE? I find every pair of my areas that share a stretch of boundary, then take the difference in HLE between the two. Areas that meet only at a single corner point don’t count. Areas vary a lot in size, so this isn’t the same as “a short walk apart”. It’s a good first look.

Show code (31 lines)
boundaries_by_code = boundaries.set_index(boundary_code_field).to_crs(27700)  # metres, so shared edge length means something
area_codes = list(boundaries_by_code.index)
shapes = boundaries_by_code.geometry.values
tree = boundaries_by_code.sindex

touching = set()

for i, shape in enumerate(shapes):
    for j in tree.query(shape, predicate="intersects"):
        # Only count areas that share at least a metre of boundary, not areas that meet at a single corner point.
        if i != j and shape.boundary.intersection(shapes[j].boundary).length > 1:
            touching.add(tuple(sorted((area_codes[i], area_codes[j]))))

assert not (set(area_codes) - {code for pair in touching for code in pair}), "An area with no neighbour needs a look"

records = []

# Sorted, so tied differences always come out in the same order.

4. What I’m taking forward

  • The average of men and women is the headline outcome. The two correlate at 0.97 across areas, so separate analyses would repeat each other. I keep the male and female figures for a final check, because the gap between them reaches 4 years one way and 7.5 the other. The average is my own derived measure, not an ONS statistic.
  • HLE in years is the outcome. The proportion of life in good health is too closely tied to HLE to be a separate measure.
  • Every estimate keeps its interval. I show the margin of error whenever I compare two areas, and I don’t rank areas that are close together.
  • The nine imputed estimates stay in, but flagged. I’ll check later whether my results hold without them.
  • No HLE estimates are removed from the data. None are on ONS’s implausible list. The highest and lowest values look believable and show up for both sexes.
  • A few areas get flagged. Student-dominated areas and city and town centres with very high crime rates are flagged in Notebook 02. Only the crime figures for the city and town centres are set aside, and every other measure is kept for every area. Birmingham city centre also gets a caveat of its own: it isn't like anywhere else. Their HLE rows stay in the data.

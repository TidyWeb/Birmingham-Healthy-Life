Notebook 04 — Hypothesis and Neighbour Gaps

Notebook 01 showed me something I couldn't let go of. Areas that share a boundary can differ in healthy life expectancy by 14 to 16 years. But every HLE figure is an estimate with a margin of error, and two estimates can look far apart just by chance. So before I build anything on these gaps, I need to know they're real.

That's the question here. Are neighbouring areas really different, or could estimate noise alone produce gaps like these? I write it down as a hypothesis, test it on every neighbour pair, and then pick out the biggest gaps with a rule I fix before I look.

I use my average of male and female HLE throughout, and check the two sexes once near the end.

3. Who counts as a neighbour

Two areas are neighbours if they share a stretch of boundary longer than one metre. Touching at a single corner doesn't count. It's the same rule as Notebook 01, and it gives 1,885 pairs. Each pair is recorded once.

touching = set()

4. The hypothesis

I write this down before testing anything.

H₀ (the null hypothesis): neighbouring areas don't really differ. Their true HLE is the same, and the gaps I see come only from estimate noise.

H₁ (the alternative): neighbouring areas differ by more than noise can produce.

I test it on all 1,885 pairs, not just the dramatic ones. Picking out the biggest gaps comes later, on purpose, but it mustn't be what decides the test.

For each pair I measure the gap two ways. In years, which is easy to picture. And in standard errors, which says how big the gap is compared with the uncertainty in the two estimates. That second one is z: the gap divided by the combined margin of error of the two areas, z = |HLE_a − HLE_b| / √(se_a² + se_b²). The usual line is 1.96. If the gaps were pure noise, only about 5% of pairs would go past it.

There's a catch. The interval for my average comes from combining the male and female intervals, and it's probably a bit too narrow (Notebook 03 explains why). Narrower intervals mean bigger z values, so this test leans slightly towards finding real differences. I also have to assume the errors in neighbouring areas are independent, because ONS doesn't publish how they're related. I keep both in mind when I read the result.

5. What pure noise would look like

To make the comparison properly, I simulate it. I pretend H₀ is true: every area has the same real HLE, and each estimate wobbles at random by an amount set by its own margin of error. Then I measure the neighbour gaps in that pretend world. I do it 10,000 times.

One detail matters. Each area sits in several pairs, so in each run I give each area one random error and reuse it in every pair it belongs to. That keeps the pairs linked the way they really are, instead of pretending each pair is a separate experiment.

Show code (48 lines)
# Array position of each area, so a pair can look up its two members' simulated errors.
ordered_codes = area_table.index.to_list()
code_positions = {code:i for i,code in enumerate(ordered_codes)}
a_index = pairs["area_a"].map(code_positions).to_numpy()
b_index = pairs["area_b"].map(code_positions).to_numpy()
area_se = area_table["se_average"].to_numpy()
se_gap = pairs["se_gap_years"].to_numpy()

# Seeded generator, so the simulated errors are the same on every run.
rng = np.random.default_rng(NOISE_SEED)
simulated_shares = np.empty(NOISE_RUNS)
simulated_medians = np.empty(NOISE_RUNS)

noise_z_counts = np.zeros(len(z_bins)-1, dtype=np.int64)

# Simulate in batches of 200 runs to keep memory small.
for start in range(0, NOISE_RUNS, 200):
    stop = min(start + 200, NOISE_RUNS)

    # One error per area per run, scaled by that area's own standard error. An area in several pairs reuses the same error in each, which keeps the dependence between pairs.
    errors = rng.normal(size=(stop-start, len(area_se))) * area_se
    noise_gaps = np.abs(errors[:,a_index] - errors[:,b_index])
    noise_z = noise_gaps / se_gap
    simulated_shares[start:stop] = (noise_z > Z_CUTOFF).mean(axis=1)
    simulated_medians[start:stop] = np.median(noise_gaps, axis=1)

    # Keep histogram counts rather than every simulated gap, so memory stays small.
    noise_gap_counts += np.histogram(noise_gaps, bins=gap_bins)[0]
    noise_z_counts += np.histogram(noise_z, bins=z_bins)[0]

Real gaps against noise

Under pure noise, 5.0% of pairs pass the 1.96 line, and in 95% of runs the share stays between 3.5% and 6.6%. The real figure is 69.8%. The typical noise gap is 0.50 years (0.46 to 0.54 across runs). The real typical gap is 2.85 years, more than five times bigger.

So I reject H₀. Neighbouring areas really do differ in healthy life expectancy, far beyond anything estimate noise can produce. That doesn't say why. It says the gaps are worth explaining.

A note to myself about that 5.0%. It isn't evidence of anything. Under H₀, z behaves like a standard normal by construction, so roughly 5% past 1.96 is guaranteed. It only shows my simulation code works. The evidence is the 69.8%.

6. Do neighbours resemble each other?

This is a different question. The test above asks whether neighbours differ. This one asks whether, across the whole map, areas tend to look like their neighbours: high next to high, low next to low.

Moran's I measures that. It compares each area's HLE with the average of its neighbours'. 0 means no pattern. Positive means neighbours tend to be alike. To check the result isn't chance, I shuffle the HLE values at random around the map 999 times and see how often a shuffled map scores as high. I wrote the calculation myself in NumPy, following the PySAL Moran implementation, rather than adding a spatial statistics package.

Show code (50 lines)
n_areas = len(area_table)

# Symmetric 0/1 matrix: entry (i, j) is 1 when areas i and j are neighbours.
adjacency = np.zeros((n_areas,n_areas),dtype=float)
adjacency[a_index,b_index] = adjacency[b_index,a_index] = 1
assert np.array_equal(adjacency,adjacency.T) and np.diag(adjacency).sum() == 0
assert adjacency.sum() == 2 * len(pairs)
degree = adjacency.sum(axis=1)
assert (degree > 0).all()

# Row-standardise: each area's neighbours share a total weight of one.
weights = adjacency / degree[:,None]
assert np.allclose(weights.sum(axis=1),1)
values = area_table["hle_average"].to_numpy()
centred = values - values.mean()
denominator = float(centred @ centred)
assert denominator > 0

# Moran's I = (n / sum of weights) × (centred values · weighted neighbour values) / sum of squared centred values.
moran_i = float(n_areas / weights.sum() * (centred @ weights @ centred) / denominator)

# I cross-check the numerator by summing the two directed weights of each undirected pair.
edge_numerator = np.sum(centred[a_index] * centred[b_index] * (1/degree[a_index] + 1/degree[b_index]))
assert np.isclose(moran_i,n_areas / weights.sum() * edge_numerator / denominator)

# Shuffle the HLE values across the fixed map. This keeps the neighbour structure and breaks any spatial pattern.
moran_rng = np.random.default_rng(MORAN_SEED)
permutation_i = np.empty(MORAN_PERMUTATIONS)

for start in range(0,MORAN_PERMUTATIONS,100):
    stop = min(start+100,MORAN_PERMUTATIONS)
    shuffled = np.stack([moran_rng.permutation(centred) for _ in range(stop-start)])
    permutation_i[start:stop] = n_areas / weights.sum() * np.sum(shuffled * (shuffled @ weights.T),axis=1) / denominator

# One-sided: the share of shuffles at least as clustered as the observed map. The +1 counts the observed value itself.
moran_positive_reference = float((1 + np.sum(permutation_i >= moran_i)) / (MORAN_PERMUTATIONS + 1))
moran_reference = np.quantile(permutation_i,[.025,.975])

display(pd.Series({"Observed Moran's I":moran_i,"Permutation mean":permutation_i.mean(),
    "Random-arrangement expectation":-1/(n_areas-1),"Permutation lower reference":moran_reference[0],
    "Permutation upper reference":moran_reference[1],"Positive-association permutation reference":moran_positive_reference}).round(4).to_frame("Value"))

What it shows

Moran's I is 0.576. The shuffled maps average about zero (−0.001), and none of the 999 reached 0.576.

So two things are true at once. Across the map, areas strongly resemble their neighbours: lower-HLE areas sit together and so do higher ones. And yet some neighbours are 15 years apart. The big gaps look like the edges, where a healthier patch meets a less healthy one.

It matters later too. Areas next to each other aren't independent, so when I test a model I have to keep neighbouring areas together. Otherwise the model gets marked on areas it has, in effect, already seen.

7. Picking out the biggest gaps

Now I go for the extremes on purpose, because they're what make the question vivid. The rule, fixed before I look:

A big-gap pair is a neighbour pair whose gap is in the top 5% of all neighbour gaps and whose z is past 1.96.

Picking extremes has a known catch. The biggest gaps include pairs where noise happened to push the two estimates further apart, so they overstate the real gap a little. Anything I find about these pairs is about these pairs, not about neighbours in general.

Nothing is dropped. If either area is a student area, a visitor hub, has an imputed estimate or carries the city-centre caveat, the pair is flagged and kept.

# Imputation is recorded per sex, so either sex in either area counts.

9. A check on men and women

This is the only place I use the two sexes separately. If my average were hiding something, I'd expect pairs where men's gap goes one way and women's the other. So for every pair with a gap past the noise line, I check whether the male and female gaps point the same way.

What this tells me

The gaps are real. Neighbouring areas differ in healthy life expectancy far more than estimate noise can explain: 69.8% of pairs are past the noise line, where pure chance would give about 5%. The typical gap is under 3 years, but the biggest are 14 to 16, and in those pairs the margins of error don't come close to overlapping.

The map has a shape as well. Areas strongly resemble their neighbours (Moran's I 0.576), so the big gaps are the exceptions. They look like the edges, where a healthier patch meets a less healthy one.

What I'm taking forward

  • The 95 big-gap pairs, picked by a rule I fixed in advance. They're my way into the question of why.
  • Neighbours aren't independent. Any model I test has to keep neighbouring areas together.
  • The average holds for both sexes. Male and female gaps point the same way in about 99% of pairs.

Limits, briefly

My interval for the average is probably a bit narrow, which makes z a bit big. Pairs that share an area aren't independent, which is why I simulate errors per area and don't give each pair its own p-value. Picking the extremes overstates the typical gap. And all of this is about areas, not people, and about what the estimates show, not why.

Notebook 03 — Joined Table and Exploration

Notebook 02 left me with eleven separate files, each describing my 660 areas from a different angle: health, income, housing, work, education, air, crime and who lives there. Here I join them into one table, one row per area, so I can put HLE side by side with everything else.

Then I start looking. Which measures move with healthy life expectancy, and how strongly? Which ones are really the same thing measured twice? By the end I want a short list, one measure for each theme, that I can take into the machine learning without counting the same idea several times over.

This notebook looks and describes. It doesn't test or model anything.

The periods differ. HLE covers 2019 to 2023, the Census measures are from March 2021, income is the year to March 2023, and pollution, fuel poverty and crime are from 2024.

Ground rules

  • All 660 areas stay in. A student flag is a caveat, not a reason to remove an area.
  • For the six visitor hubs, only the crime figure used for analysis is blanked. The published crime and anti-social behaviour rates stay as they are.
  • I use the saved flags exactly as Notebook 02 set them: the 50% student rule and the crime outlier fence aren't changed here.
  • Every join matches area codes one to one.
  • I keep all 19 ethnic-group shares and decide later how to use them.

2. HLE, with its margins of error

I start from the fuller HLE table Notebook 02 saved, because it keeps the confidence limits and the imputation flags. I turn it from two rows per area (male and female) into one row holding both, and check the values still match the compact table. Nine male estimates had some inputs filled in by ONS. No female ones did. All nine stay in, flagged.

Why one average for men and women

Male and female HLE track each other very closely across my areas, a correlation of 0.97 (Notebook 01). Running everything twice would mostly give me the same answer twice. So my headline outcome is the simple average of the two, hle_average = (male + female) / 2. I keep the male and female figures for a final check that the average isn't hiding a difference between the sexes. The average is my own measure, not an ONS one.

It needs a margin of error too. Each published 95% interval gives a standard error for that sex, (UCI − LCI) / (2 × 1.96), and I combine the two as √(SE_male² + SE_female²) / 2. That assumes the male and female errors are independent. They probably aren't quite, since both come from the same area and years, so the interval for the average is likely a little too narrow. I treat it as a guide.

# Standard error of each sex from its published 95% interval: full width divided by 2 × 1.96.
se_male = (wide["hle_male_uci"] - wide["hle_male_lci"]) / (2 * 1.96)
se_female = (wide["hle_female_uci"] - wide["hle_female_lci"]) / (2 * 1.96)

# The headline outcome: the mean of the two sexes. It is derived here, not published by ONS.
wide["hle_average"] = (wide["hle_male"] + wide["hle_female"]) / 2
se_average = np.sqrt(se_male**2 + se_female**2) / 2  # assumes the two sexes' errors are independent
wide["hle_average_lci"] = wide["hle_average"] - 1.96 * se_average
wide["hle_average_uci"] = wide["hle_average"] + 1.96 * se_average
assert wide["hle_average_lci"].lt(wide["hle_average"]).all() and wide["hle_average"].lt(wide["hle_average_uci"]).all()
source_for_column.update({c: "Derived in this notebook from the male and female HLE columns" for c in ["hle_average","hle_average_lci","hle_average_uci"]})
wide["hle_period"] = "2019–2023"

4. The flags, and the six crime blanks

Every join matched all 660 areas. Now the flags from Notebook 02. I bring in only the flags, because the flags file also repeats the crime rate and I don't want two copies of it.

For the six visitor hubs I blank the crime figure in a separate analysis column, crime_for_analysis. Their published crime rate stays in its own column, and every other measure stays in. Two areas are both student areas and visitor hubs. Their crime figure is blank because they're hubs.

7. Statistical ideas in plain words

The ideas I lean on from here on, with my own data as the example.

  • Mean. Add the values up and divide by how many there are. Average HLE across my areas is 63.9 years. A few extreme areas can drag it up or down.
  • Median. Put the areas in order and take the middle one. Half are below it, half above. It's 64.1 years here, and it barely moves if a few areas are extreme.
  • Standard deviation. How spread out the values are around the mean, in the same units. For HLE it's 5.1 years, so a typical area sits about 5 years from the average. It's the spread between areas, not the uncertainty in one area's estimate.
  • Variance. The standard deviation squared, so it's in years squared, which is hard to picture. I mostly use the standard deviation. pandas divides by n − 1, the usual formula for a sample.
  • Probability. How likely something is, from 0 (never) to 1 (certain). My 660 areas aren't a random sample. They're the areas I chose to study, so I'm careful about treating them as if they were.
  • Hypothesis testing. Start with a claim to test, such as "the gaps between neighbours are only estimate noise". Work out what the data would look like if that were true, and compare it with what I actually see. A p-value says how surprising the real result would be if the claim were true. It isn't the chance that the claim is true.
  • Spearman correlation. Do areas that rank high on one measure also rank high (or low) on another? It runs from −1 to +1 and doesn't need the relationship to be a straight line. A strong correlation doesn't mean one thing causes the other.

These ideas are the foundation for everything after this. The mean, median and standard deviation describe the data before I trust any pattern in it. Probability and hypothesis testing let me ask whether a pattern is bigger than chance alone would produce. And correlation tells me where to look next, but never why.

Choosing one measure per theme

They overlap a lot. NO₂ and PM2.5 are almost the same map (0.97). Deprivation and income are −0.94. Deprivation and no qualifications are 0.93, and overcrowding and unemployment 0.92. A lot of my measures are one story, the money story, told several ways.

So I pick a short list. I start with household income after housing costs as the anchor for the money story, because it has no health element in it, so it can't partly repeat HLE. Then I go through the other measures from strongest HLE link to weakest. Any measure that correlates at 0.80 or more with one I've already picked becomes an alternative, not a second pick. The 0.80 line is my working rule for "too similar", not a statistical test.

Two measures are set aside before the rule runs. Household deprivation has the strongest link of all with HLE, but one of its four dimensions is health and disability. Using health to predict health would be cheating, so it stays in the table for description only. Level 2 qualifications is the middle band and barely links with HLE (about −0.17 averaged across the three outcomes), so it isn't shortlisted.

I picked this list by looking at the same data I'll model, so it's a starting point to check later, not a finished answer.

What this tells me

HLE varies a lot across my 660 areas. The average of men and women runs from 53.7 to 75.7 years, a range of 22 years, with a mean of 63.9 and a standard deviation of 5.1. For men it's 52.4 to 75.4, and for women 52.9 to 76.6.

And it lines up with money more than anything else. Household deprivation (−0.95) and income after housing costs (+0.94) rank the areas almost exactly the way HLE does, for men and women alike. The housing, work and education measures follow close behind, and the overlap checks show why: they're mostly the same story. Crime is a little weaker, and pollution and fuel poverty weaker again.

That's a correlation across areas, picked out after looking at a lot of measures. It doesn't show that money is the cause, and it says nothing about any one person.

The margins of error are worth remembering. The median 95% interval is 3.0 years wide for men and 2.8 for women. For my average it's narrower, about 2.1 years, partly because it combines two estimates and partly because it's probably a little too narrow. Two areas a year apart can't really be told apart.

What I'm taking forward

  • One outcome: the average of male and female HLE, with the two sexes kept for a check.
  • Seven measures: income, Level 4 qualifications, recorded crime, fuel poverty, NO₂, employment and anti-social behaviour, with the alternatives held in reserve.
  • Household deprivation stays out of any model, because it contains health.
  • Ethnic composition is undecided. All 19 shares stay in the table.

Limits, briefly

The measures come from different years, and pollution, fuel poverty and crime are from after the HLE period. Neighbouring areas are related, so I don't treat them as independent. Crime rates are swollen in town centres, which is why the six hubs are blanked. These are areas, not people.
